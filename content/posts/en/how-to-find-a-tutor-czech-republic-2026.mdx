## How much does a tutor cost in the Czech Republic?

Private Czech lessons in Prague usually cost 350 to 575 CZK an hour, roughly $15 to $25, while structured group courses start much lower, from around 110 CZK per 45-minute lesson ([Language International](https://www.languageinternational.com/czech-courses-czech-republic), [AmazingTalker](https://en.amazingtalker.com/tutor-price/czech), 2026).

![Tutor and student studying together at a table with notebooks](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6929206/pexels-photo-6929206.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

The gap between the two makes sense once you know what you're paying for. A one-on-one lesson is built entirely around you, your pace, your mistakes, your specific reason for learning. A group course is cheaper because the teacher's attention is split ten ways. Neither is objectively better, it depends on whether you need flexibility or structure.

Online tutoring often sits somewhere in between, since the tutor isn't losing travel time and can teach more hours in a day. That savings sometimes gets passed on, but not always, so it's worth asking directly rather than assuming.

## Where do expats actually find tutors in Prague?

Most expats find a tutor through an informal channel first, a colleague's recommendation, a neighbour, or a Facebook group post, not a dedicated platform. That's not because they have nobody Czech to ask.

Many expats do have Czech colleagues, neighbours, or friends they could turn to. The real friction is different: nobody wants to message the same person for the third time this year asking if they know a tutor, a plumber, or a dentist. Facebook groups fill that gap, but scrolling through years-old threads and messaging five people who never reply back gets tiring fast.

## Does a tutor need a license in the Czech Republic?

A tutor who teaches regularly and charges for it is generally expected to hold a trade license, a živnostenský list. EU citizens can teach directly without one, while non-EU residents typically need to register first ([expats.cz](https://www.expats.cz/czech-news/article/how-to-get-a-trade-license-zivnostensky-list-to-do-business-in-the-czech-republic), [TEFL Org](https://www.tefl.org/teach-english-abroad/teach-english-in-czech-republic/), 2026).

This matters for you as the person hiring, not only for the tutor. It's a basic signal that someone treats tutoring as a real, accountable activity rather than an occasional cash favour. It doesn't guarantee quality, but a tutor who's registered and can point you to their trade record is at least operating in the open.

You can check any registered business for free on [rzp.gov.cz](https://rzp.gov.cz) or via ARES, it takes seconds and shows whether the person is actually registered to provide the service they're offering.

## How do you tell a good tutor from a risky one?

Reviews matter more than most people assume. 93% of consumers read reviews before trusting a local business or service provider ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey-2025/), 2025), and tutoring is no different, a tutor with zero trace online, no reviews, no profile, no history, is a genuine unknown.

A few checks take almost no time and remove most of the risk. Ask for a trial lesson before committing to a package. Check whether their profile or listing has any reviews at all, even two or three honest ones tell you something. If they're claiming a formal qualification for academic subjects, it's fair to ask to see it.

## Online tutor or in person? What actually works better?

There's no single right answer, it depends on the subject and who's learning. In-person lessons tend to work better for younger children and hands-on subjects like music, where a teacher needs to see and correct posture or technique in real time.

![Person taking an online lesson on a laptop at home](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6502728/pexels-photo-6502728.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

Online lessons suit busy adults learning Czech for daily life or work, since there's no commute and scheduling is more flexible. Isn't the real question whether you'll actually show up consistently? A slightly less ideal format you stick with beats a perfect one you keep rescheduling.

## What should you ask before the first lesson?

A short list of questions upfront avoids most of the awkward surprises later. Ask about the hourly rate and whether it changes for packages, the cancellation policy, what materials are included, and whether they teach in English for early lessons if your Czech isn't there yet.

For younger learners or academic subjects, ask directly about relevant experience with that age group or curriculum. None of this needs to feel like an interrogation, a tutor who's done this before will expect the questions and answer them without hesitation.

**Do I need to find a tutor with a trade license?**

Ideally yes, if they tutor regularly and charge for it. EU citizens can teach directly, non-EU residents generally need a živnostenský list first ([expats.cz](https://www.expats.cz/czech-news/article/how-to-get-a-trade-license-zivnostensky-list-to-do-business-in-the-czech-republic), 2026). **Is it normal to find a tutor through a Facebook group?**

Very common, and it works. The friction isn't a lack of contacts, it's the repetition of asking the same people or scrolling old threads every time you need something new.

**How do I know if a tutor is legitimate?**

Check for any reviews, ask for a trial lesson, and confirm pricing and qualifications upfront. 93% of consumers read reviews before trusting a local provider, so a complete absence of any online trace is worth noticing ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey-2025/), 2025).
