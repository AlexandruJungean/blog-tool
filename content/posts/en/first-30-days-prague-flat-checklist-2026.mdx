The lease is signed, the keys are in hand, and somewhere in the next few weeks a handful of quiet deadlines start ticking. Some are legal. Some are just the kind of thing that causes real friction if it slips, a mailbox nobody can find your name on, a utility bill that never got transferred.

None of it is complicated once you know the order to do it in. This is the checklist, split into what actually has a deadline attached and what can reasonably wait.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Foreigners must report their residence address within 3 working days of moving in, unless the landlord already filed it via the UBYPORT system; missing this carries a fine up to 3,000 CZK
> - A signed handover protocol with meter readings is what protects you from being billed for a previous tenant's consumption or blamed for pre-existing damage
> - Utility transfers typically take 10 to 30 days, so start in week one, it's the slowest item on the list
> - Smoke detectors are legally required only in buildings approved after 2008, and an unmarked mailbox means Czech Post won't deliver, and won't take a complaint about it either

## What has to happen in the first 3 working days?

Foreigners must report their place of residence to the Police of the Czech Republic within 3 working days of moving in, unless the landlord or accommodation provider already filed it electronically through the UBYPORT system on the tenant's behalf (Police of the Czech Republic). Missing this deadline is a genuine offence, with a fine of up to 3,000 CZK.

![Moving boxes and keys laid out in a new apartment](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7489107/pexels-photo-7489107.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

In practice, ask your landlord first. Many landlords and letting agents already register every new tenant through UBYPORT as a matter of routine, since it's a fast electronic form on their end. If they haven't, or you're not sure, you can file it yourself at the police foreigner registration office with your lease and ID. It takes a few minutes and closes off any risk of the fine.

## What should the handover protocol actually cover?

A written handover protocol (předávací protokol) recording the electricity and gas meter readings, their serial numbers, the date, and both parties' names is what protects you from being billed for a previous tenant's consumption, and from disputes over damage that was already there when you arrived.

A verbal agreement or a few phone photos aren't a substitute. If a dispute comes up later, about a bill, about a scratch on a floor, the signed protocol is the only thing that settles it cleanly. This also connects directly to a separate question worth understanding early: [which repairs are legally the landlord's job, and which are yours](/apartment-maintenance-landlord-vs-yours-2026), since the same document is often the reference point when that question comes up.

## How do I transfer utilities into my name?

Transferring an existing electricity or gas contract typically takes 10 to 30 days, which makes it the slowest-moving item on this entire list, and the best reason to start it in week one rather than week three.

You'll need the handover protocol's meter readings, proof of your right to the flat (the lease itself usually works), and often a landlord's power of attorney if you're keeping the existing supplier rather than switching. Most suppliers let you complete this online or with a joint visit by the outgoing and incoming tenant. If you'd rather switch suppliers entirely, that's a separate, slightly longer process, worth starting even earlier.

## Is a smoke detector required in my flat?

Smoke detectors have been legally required in Czechia only for buildings approved after 2008, with an additional unit required if a flat's floor area exceeds 150 m² (Government Decree 23/2008 Sb.). Buildings completed before that date generally have no retroactive obligation to install one.

That means an older Prague building, and there are plenty of them, may genuinely have no detector and no legal requirement to add one. It's still worth installing your own. They're inexpensive, and a landlord in an older building has no obligation to provide one for you.

## The small setup details that get missed

A handful of small tasks cause disproportionate friction if they're skipped in week one, mostly because nobody thinks to mention them until something goes wrong.

Label your mailbox. Czech Post will not deliver to, and will not accept a complaint about, a mailbox without your name on it. This one is easy to forget and genuinely causes missed mail, including registered letters you actually need.

## FAQ

**Do I have to register my address as a foreigner in Czechia?**

Yes. You must report your residence to the Police of the Czech Republic within 3 working days of moving in, unless your landlord already filed it through the UBYPORT system. Missing the deadline risks a fine of up to 3,000 CZK.

**What is a handover protocol and do I actually need one?**

It's a written, signed record of the meter readings, serial numbers, and the flat's condition at move-in. It's what protects you later if there's a dispute over a utility bill or existing damage, a verbal agreement or a few photos won't settle that argument the same way.

**How long does it take to transfer electricity and gas into my name?**

Typically 10 to 30 days, which is why it's worth starting in week one rather than waiting. It's the slowest-moving task on this checklist by a wide margin.

**Do I need a smoke detector in my flat?**

Legally, only if the building was approved after 2008 (Government Decree 23/2008 Sb.), with an extra unit required over 150 m² of floor area. Older buildings have no retroactive requirement, but a detector is cheap and worth installing regardless.

**Why isn't my mail arriving?**

Czech Post will not deliver to a mailbox without a name on it, and won't process a delivery complaint about one either. Labelling your mailbox in week one avoids missed mail entirely.
